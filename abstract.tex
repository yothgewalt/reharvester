\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Keeping pace with a fast-growing research literature is increasingly difficult, and
the cloud services that promise to help are opaque, non-portable, and demand that
sensitive reading lists leave the user's machine. We present \emph{Reharvester}, a
local-first system that turns a keyword or abstract query into an interactive map of a
research field without any external dependency. Reharvester harvests papers, assembles
them into a navigable knowledge graph, surfaces emerging keyword trends, and exposes the
underlying corpus through a wiki-linked reader backed by retrieval-augmented generation
over the graph. The entire pipeline runs on the user's hardware and degrades gracefully
when compute or models are unavailable, making literature exploration reproducible,
private, and air-gappable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The volume of published research outpaces any individual's capacity to read, and
existing discovery tools centralize both the data and the reasoning in the cloud. This
is a poor fit for privacy-sensitive or offline settings, and it hides the ranking and
retrieval logic from the researcher. Reharvester reframes literature discovery as a
local, inspectable pipeline: every stage---ingestion, graph construction, trend
analysis, and question answering---executes on the user's own machine and remains open
to inspection.

\section{Approach}
Reharvester couples four stages. \textbf{Ingestion} gates a harvest on a meaningful
query---at least five keywords or a short abstract---then crawls matching papers while
streaming progress and a live terminal log to the user. \textbf{Graph construction}
builds a mutual $k$-nearest-neighbour backbone over TF--IDF cosine similarity, partitions
it into topical communities with Louvain modularity, and scores every paper by PageRank
centrality and by a bridge score---the fraction of its edge weight crossing a community
boundary---which operationalises the structural-hole intuition at document level.
\textbf{Trend analysis} ranks keywords by the regression slope
of their frequency over a user-adjustable multi-year window, distinguishing durable
themes from noise, and presents them beside a coarse-grained field map that aggregates
the backbone to its communities: one node per community above a size threshold, edges
for the inter-community links above a count threshold, the largest labelled.
\textbf{Corpus access} presents the corpus as a three-pane reader:
the communities form a navigable index, the centre pane renders one paper with its
metadata and an Obsidian-style \texttt{[[wikilink]]} abstract, and its nearest
neighbours are listed along the backbone with their cosine similarities, so navigation
follows semantic adjacency rather than a ranked list. A local language model answers
questions through retrieval-augmented generation grounded in that graph.

\section{Implementation and Results}
The system is implemented as a Go harvesting server and a Next.js~16 single-page
interface using Zustand for client-global state that survives navigation, so crawls and
selections persist across views. The interface is deliberately plain DOM: an earlier
WebGL rendering of the whole graph was replaced by the three-pane reader, which removes
a heavyweight rendering dependency and, more importantly, matches how the corpus is
actually read---one paper at a time, following the backbone---rather than viewed as a
whole. The question-answering panel exposes its own context set, labelling each admitted
document as a retrieval seed or a graph expansion and reporting the word budget it was
assembled under, so the retrieval decision is auditable rather than hidden behind the
generated prose. Resilience is built in throughout: health polling with automatic
recovery, a capability-tier indicator, and an explicit model-unreachable state in which
the assembled context is still returned and presented as the result. Together these
choices deliver a private, portable, and responsive tool for exploring a research field
end to end.
